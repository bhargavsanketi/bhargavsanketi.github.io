Bhargav D Sanketi, PhD

How tissues build themselves. How they break. How to rewrite them.

I study tissue state.

Tissues are not fixed by their genes. They move between states to get things done. They build during development. They repair after injury. They drift in disease and age.

Those shifts run on mechanics, signaling, transcription, metabolism, and chromatin. They set whether a tissue builds itself up or breaks itself down.

I have spent ten years learning to read those decisions and rewrite them. I read tissue state with single cell and spatial genomics, lineage tracing, and imaging. I rewrite it with protein, gene, and cell based tools.

The circuits that build an organ can be redeployed to restore it.

Cornell2017 to 2023

Building tissues

I asked how the gut gets its shape.

The mammalian intestine loops and layers itself early, and when that goes wrong it causes congenital malformation and metabolic disease. We built tools to measure and move mechanical forces inside a living embryo. We found that rotation starts when local mechanical feedback amplifies the body plan. An organ can begin to build itself from a local source of asymmetry.

Then we built a single cell atlas of the developing intestine and traced the origin of its fat absorption machinery. Villus myofibroblasts turned out to be the progenitors of gut lymphatic musculature, in the embryo and in the adult.

Developmental programs are modular. They get reused far beyond the organ that first deploys them.

Altos Labs2023 to 2026

Breaking tissues

I turned to how tissues fail.

Across more than forty human age associated diseases, cells near injury drift toward a mesenchymal state. We defined that drift and showed partial reprogramming reverses it.

Reprogramming rejuvenates cells, but left running it erases their identity and forms tumors. We separated the two. The chromatin activity that rejuvenates can be uncoupled from the identity loss. That makes epigenetic reprogramming safer to pursue as a medicine.

We also showed that cell identity factors reverse state drift and restore function in aging and diseased muscle and liver in live animals.

Fibrosis, cancer, and aging are miscued state transitions. The circuitry that builds an organ can be redirected to restore one.
